Every distributed system fails at networking. When it fails, there are only two outs: showing possibly stale data or showing nothing. That sentence is the entire CAP theorem in practice — hiding the detail almost nobody explains: **partition tolerance (P) isn't an option, it's the prerequisite** enabling the consistency (C) vs availability (A) choice. And with no failure, PACELC enters: latency or consistency?

## 1. Context

**CAP (Brewer, 2000):** consistency (every read sees the newest data), availability (every request gets a response), partition tolerance (keeps running with the network split). **ACID** (traditional SQL): atomic, consistent, isolated, durable transactions — precision first. **BASE** (distributed NoSQL): basically available, soft state, eventually consistent — availability and scale before immediate precision.

### C, A, P — one by one, before comparing

**Consistency: every read sees the latest write, on any node.** You write in the US server and read in Brazil: same data, no "not replicated yet". Price: writes wait for (nearly) everyone's confirmation — slow, and stuck if someone doesn't answer. Think bank transfers: zeroed balance here denies the withdrawal there, always equal.

**Availability: every request gets a response.** Success, error, 404 — but a response, never silence or endless waiting. Price: answers may come from stale replicas. Think feeds: seconds-old photos, page always up.

**Partition tolerance: keeps running with the network split.** Partition = communication failure between nodes (cable, provider, deploy, zone outage). Tolerant systems keep serving on each side of the cut — even if sides temporarily diverge. It's not "having many parts": it's **surviving the cut**. Since networks always fail (at scale, it's when, not if), P isn't chosen — it's assumed.

## 2. The problem

Global e-commerce: catalog in the US, replica in Brazil. A product is deleted in the US; replication fails midway. The Brazilian user opens the storefront. Two outs, no middle ground:

1. **Show the product** (possibly deleted) → availability: smooth UX, risk of selling what doesn't exist.
2. **Show unavailable/loading** until synced → consistency: correct data, store "broken" for minutes.

Not deciding is deciding badly: every silent database default chooses for you. The operational question CAP summarizes: *in a partition, stale data or nothing?*

## 3. Mental model

**P is prerequisite, not an option.** Without multiple nodes there are no partitions — and without partitions there's no theorem (a single database is "CA" vacuously, not by choice). In real distributed systems, P is fixed: the choice is always **C vs A, during the failure**. Teaching "freely pick 2 of 3" is the mistake confusing most interviews.

**CP: when wrong data costs money.** Tickets (two people must not buy the same seat), airlines, inventory (ten buyers, one last unit?), finance (zeroed balance denies the second transfer). MongoDB, etcd/Consul, CockroachDB.

**AP: almost everything else.** Feed, profile, dashboard, comments, search: a seconds-stale photo breaks nothing — a down page does. DynamoDB, Cassandra, CouchDB.

**Per service, not per system.** Microservices apply CAP per segment: CP ticket booking (non-negotiable consistency) + AP movie search (stale descriptions for seconds are fine). Decide at foundation design, before architecture — later it becomes rework.

**Brewer revisited (2012): spectrum, not binary.** Consistency and availability come in degrees; partitions are rare in most workloads — outside them, C and A can be optimized together. "Pick 2" became a starting point, not law.

**PACELC (Abadi, 2010): and without failure?** With partitions: A or C (same as CAP). **Else**: **Latency or Consistency**. DynamoDB = PA/EL (available in failure, fast normally); Spanner = PC/EC (always consistent, paying latency with TrueTime):

![Diagram: with partition A vs C; without partition L vs C](/img/diagramas/pacelc-fluxo-en.svg)

**Rule of thumb.** In partitions: stale data or nothing? Without partitions: fast or identical everywhere? Two questions, four combinations — each service answers separately.

### Distributing means replicating, sharding — or both (NoSQL Distilled)

Two independent axes ([Fowler, key points](https://martinfowler.com/)): **sharding** (different data on different servers — each the single source of its slice) and **replication** (same data in several places). Replication has two flavors: **leader-follower** (writes on the leader, fewer conflicts, single write point) and **peer-to-peer** (writes on any node, no single point, reconcile later — Dynamo/Cassandra).

Three consequences closing the post: strong consistency doesn't require talking to *every* replica — a sufficient **quorum** does; consistency costs latency (more nodes per operation, more waiting); and **polyglot persistence** is the honest conclusion — Postgres at the transactional core, Redis for cache, Dynamo for feeds — with the cost warning: every new technology charges operational complexity. Picking databases means marrying latency, model, and operations per workload, as section 4 already does.

### The 4-line proof (Gilbert & Lynch)

Why can't all three hold at once? Two servers, `G1` and `G2`, same variable `v = v0`:

1. Partition the network: `G1` and `G2` can't talk.
2. A client writes `v1` to `G1`. Availability forces a response — `G1` acknowledges without replicating (no way to).
3. A client reads from `G2`. Availability forces a response — `G2` returns `v0` (never heard of `v1`).
4. A post-write read returned stale data: **inconsistency**. Contradiction — so simultaneous C+A+P doesn't exist.

It's the formal proof ([Gilbert & Lynch, 2002](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/564585.564587)) turned into intuition: availability forces answers, partitions block syncing, consistency foots the bill. Keep the 4 steps for the interview.

## 4. Implementation (decisions)

Pocket checklist per workload:

| Workload | Choice | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| money, seats, inventory | PC/EC | Postgres, Spanner, etcd |
| feed, search, catalog | PA/EL | DynamoDB, Cassandra |
| coordination, locks, config | PC/EC | etcd, Consul, ZooKeeper |
| cache, sessions, analytics | PA/EL | Redis cluster, SimpleDB |

Without partitions, fine-tune: Cosmos DB offers 5 levels between eventual and strong — PACELC operationalized as configuration. Implementation means picking the level per workload, not the "right" database in the abstract.

### Tunable consistency: MongoDB as example

CP/AP labels mislead when databases calibrate. In MongoDB, `writeConcern: "majority"` waits for most replicas (strong consistency, higher latency — finance, inventory); `writeConcern: 1` confirms on the primary and moves on (fast, eventual). `readPreference: "secondary"` reads from secondaries (available, maybe stale); from the primary (current, loaded). Same database, two behaviors — the "CAP choice" becomes a per-operation parameter, not a product tattoo. Chat (available, reconciles later) and banking (consistent, halts in partitions) can even live in one cluster with different concerns.

## 5. Failures and trade-offs

**"Freely pick 2".** There is no distributed CA (without P there's no distributed system). Reciting the triangle without fixed-P misses the interviewer's first question.

**ACID consistency ≠ CAP consistency.** ACID-C = respected invariants and rules; CAP-C = every read sees the latest write (linearizability). Mixing them contaminates the design. ([Kleppmann, A Critique of the CAP Theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.05393)).

**CP everywhere / AP everywhere.** AP booking sells duplicate tickets; CP search takes the store down over a description. Per-service granularity, always.

**Ignoring the Else.** Teams only debating partitions forget 99.9% of the time has no failure — and that's where latency vs consistency decides UX and cost. PACELC exists for the common case, not the exception.

**Treating CAP as a technical decision.** The consistency vs availability trade-off is a **business** decision, not engineering ([Fowler & Cartwright](https://martinfowler.com/)): a double-booking's cost may be lower than selling nothing — and demanding "five nines" even for the sandwich-ordering system is waste with an architecture stamp. Resilience (and consistency) per component, calibrated by value at risk: booking tolerates zero doubles, search tolerates seconds of staleness. Without finance in the room, architects optimize in the dark.

**Picking databases on CAP alone.** Nobody picks Cassandra for chat "because it's AP" — they pick it for distributed writes, multiregion operation, data model. As ByteByteGo summarizes quoting Brewer himself: CAP prohibits only a tiny slice of the design space (*perfect* availability *and* consistency under partitions, which are rare). The theorem opens the trade-off discussion; picking the database requires digging into latency, model, operations, and cost.
